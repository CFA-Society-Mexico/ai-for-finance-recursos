Black-Scholes-Merton, a visual explainer

The pricing formula is the headline. The Greeks (its partial derivatives) are what an options trader actually hedges. Move the sliders and watch every chart redraw, live.

Built by Eduardo Ramos, CFA

For most people, Black-Scholes ends at the price. That is where the interesting part actually starts. The same closed-form equation hides a whole family of partial derivatives, and those derivatives are what trading books are actually built around.

What follows is the model laid out as a working instrument. Six inputs at the top, the live price and Greeks immediately below, and twelve charts that show how each sensitivity deforms when you change the underlying, the volatility, or the time to expiry.

03

Delta and Gamma, the hedge

The first two derivatives with respect to spot. Delta is the hedge ratio, Gamma is how fast the hedge ratio moves. Together they govern almost every dynamic-hedging decision on a real options book.

Delta vs spot Call Put

Call delta sweeps from 0 to e−qτ as the option crosses from deep OTM to deep ITM. Put delta is its symmetric counterpart, bounded between −e−qτ and 0. The two dots mark the current spot: top dot is the Call delta right now, bottom dot is the Put.

Gamma vs spot Call Put

Gamma peaks at the money and tightens as expiry approaches. Identical for Call and Put, so the two dots sit on top of each other. This is why short-gamma books bleed on Friday afternoons.

04

Vega, Theta, Rho

The three other first-order sensitivities. Vega is exposure to volatility, Theta is time decay, Rho is sensitivity to the risk-free rate. Together with Delta they cover the linear part of the BSM gradient.

Vega vs spot Call Put

Identical for Call and Put. Peaks ATM where the option carries the most optionality. Per 1% move in σ.

Theta vs spot Call Put

The rent you pay for optionality. Negative for long options, most aggressive when the option is ATM and close to expiry. Per calendar day.

Rho vs spot Call Put

Sensitivity to the risk-free rate. Positive for Calls, negative for Puts. Becomes material on long-dated options. Per 1% move in r.

05

Cross-derivatives, on demand

Vanna, Volga, Charm. The second-order Greeks that govern how the first-order ones move when more than one input changes. Each sparkline plots the Greek across the spot axis; the dot marks the current spot, with Call Put . When the Greek is identical for Call and Put (as with Vanna and Volga) the two dots overlap.
